# Reading notes: Hopf (1951)

Eberhard Hopf, "Uber die Anfangswertaufgabe fur die hydrodynamischen Grundgleichungen," *Mathematische Nachrichten* **4** (1951), 213-231.

> Hopf is the second pillar of the global weak-solution theory. Where Leray (1934) built finite-energy "turbulent" solutions on all of $\mathbb{R}^3$ by mollification and compactness, Hopf rebuilt the existence theorem on a general (bounded) spatial domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ with the physical no-slip boundary condition $u|_{\partial\Omega}=0$, and did it by what is now called the Faedo-Galerkin method: project onto finite-dimensional divergence-free subspaces, solve the resulting ODE system, and pass to the limit using the energy bound. The output is the class the whole subject is named after, **Leray-Hopf weak solutions**, together with the **energy inequality** that is to this day the only coercive globally-in-time a priori bound. This note is squarely Architecture 1, and its primary structural tie-in is control (B): the bound Hopf proves is the energy, which is *supercritical* under the Navier-Stokes scaling, so by itself it cannot reach a regularity statement. The historical importance is that Hopf isolated, in clean functional-analytic form, exactly the bound we have and exactly the gap we still face.

## Statement

Let $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ be a domain (Hopf's primary setting is a bounded domain with sufficiently regular boundary; $d=3$ is the case of interest, though the construction is dimension-agnostic). Consider the incompressible Navier-Stokes initial-boundary-value problem

$$\partial_t u - \nu\,\Delta u + (u\cdot\nabla)u + \nabla p = f, \qquad \nabla\cdot u = 0 \ \text{ in } \Omega\times(0,T),$$
$$u|_{\partial\Omega} = 0, \qquad u|_{t=0} = u_0,$$

with viscosity $\nu > 0$, divergence-free initial datum $u_0$, and (optionally) a forcing $f$. Hopf's theorem, in modern language:

**Theorem (Hopf 1951, global existence of weak solutions).** For every $u_0 \in H$ (the closure in $L^2(\Omega)^d$ of smooth compactly-supported divergence-free vector fields) and every $T>0$, there exists a weak solution

$$u \in L^\infty\big(0,T; H\big) \cap L^2\big(0,T; V\big),$$

where $V$ is the closure of the same smooth divergence-free fields in $H^1_0(\Omega)^d$, such that $u$ satisfies the equation in the integrated (distributional) sense against divergence-free test fields $\varphi$:

$$-\int_0^T\!\!\int_\Omega u\cdot\partial_t\varphi \,dx\,dt + \nu\int_0^T\!\!\int_\Omega \nabla u : \nabla\varphi \,dx\,dt + \int_0^T\!\!\int_\Omega (u\cdot\nabla)u\cdot\varphi\,dx\,dt = \int_\Omega u_0\cdot\varphi(0)\,dx + \langle f, \varphi\rangle,$$

and which obeys the **energy inequality**

$$\tfrac12\|u(t)\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 + \nu\int_0^t \|\nabla u(s)\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2\,ds \ \le\ \tfrac12\|u_0\|_{L^2(\Omega)}^2 \qquad (\text{for } f\equiv 0, \text{ a.e. } t\in[0,T]),$$

with the obvious forcing term added when $f\neq 0$. The solution is weakly continuous in time into $L^2$, $u\in C_w([0,T];H)$, and attains the initial datum in that sense.

Two structural remarks that the modern reader should attach to the statement:

- The test class is the **divergence-free** fields. The pressure $p$ does not appear in the weak formulation: it has been eliminated by testing only against $\nabla\cdot\varphi=0$. This is the functional-analytic shadow of the **Leray projector** $\mathbb{P}$ onto divergence-free fields. Recovering $p$ afterward (as a distribution, via de Rham / the Helmholtz decomposition) is a separate step.
- The energy *inequality*, not equality. Equality is what the smooth solution would satisfy; the weak limit can only lose energy in the limit, never gain it. Whether equality in fact holds (no anomalous dissipation) is tied to regularity and to the Onsager-type threshold, and is not provided by this construction.

### Function spaces and their criticality

The two spaces in the conclusion are exactly the two halves of the energy bound:

- $L^\infty_t H = L^\infty_t L^2_x$ on the divergence-free fields. The spatial norm here is $\|u\|_{L^2}$.
- $L^2_t V \hookrightarrow L^2_t \dot H^1_x$, the time-integrated gradient bound, i.e. the dissipation $\nu\int\|\nabla u\|_{L^2}^2$.

These are the same two norms Leray controls, and the criticality verdict is identical (see below). The novelty of Hopf is not a new bound: it is that the *same* bound is achieved on a bounded domain with no-slip walls, by a *different and more flexible* construction.

## Method / structure

The proof is the template for nearly all later existence theory, so it is worth stating at the lemma level.

**1. A Galerkin basis adapted to the constraint.** Choose a countable orthonormal basis $\{w_k\}_{k\ge1}$ of $H$ consisting of divergence-free fields vanishing on $\partial\Omega$. The canonical modern choice (Ladyzhenskaya, Temam) is the eigenfunctions of the **Stokes operator** $A = -\mathbb{P}\Delta$, but the only properties used are: the $w_k$ are divergence-free, satisfy the boundary condition, and span $H$ (and $V$). The divergence-free choice is what lets the pressure drop out automatically, because the basis already lives in the kernel of $\nabla\cdot$.

**2. The Galerkin ODE system.** Seek an approximate solution in the span of the first $n$ basis fields,

$$u_n(t) = \sum_{k=1}^n g_{nk}(t)\,w_k,$$

requiring the equation to hold after projection onto $\mathrm{span}\{w_1,\dots,w_n\}$:

$$\big(\partial_t u_n,\, w_j\big) + \nu\big(\nabla u_n, \nabla w_j\big) + \big((u_n\cdot\nabla)u_n,\, w_j\big) = (f,w_j), \qquad j=1,\dots,n.$$

This is a finite system of ODEs for the coefficients $g_{nk}(t)$ with a quadratic (in the coefficients) nonlinearity coming from the convection term. Local-in-time existence of $g_{nk}$ is the Picard-Lindelof theorem.

**3. The energy estimate makes the ODE global and the family compact.** Test the projected equation against $u_n$ itself (take $\varphi = u_n$, legitimate because $u_n$ is in the span). The key structural cancellation is

$$\big((u_n\cdot\nabla)u_n,\, u_n\big)_{L^2(\Omega)} = 0,$$

which holds because $\nabla\cdot u_n = 0$ and $u_n|_{\partial\Omega}=0$ (integrate by parts; the boundary term vanishes by no-slip, the interior term vanishes by incompressibility). **This single cancellation is the entire energy method.** It says the nonlinearity does no net work in the $L^2$ inner product: convection redistributes energy among scales but neither creates nor destroys it. What remains is

$$\tfrac12\frac{d}{dt}\|u_n\|_{L^2}^2 + \nu\|\nabla u_n\|_{L^2}^2 = (f, u_n),$$

which integrates (for $f\equiv 0$) to the energy identity

$$\tfrac12\|u_n(t)\|_{L^2}^2 + \nu\int_0^t\|\nabla u_n\|_{L^2}^2 \,ds = \tfrac12\|P_n u_0\|_{L^2}^2 \le \tfrac12\|u_0\|_{L^2}^2.$$

Because the right-hand side is finite and uniform in $n$, the ODE solution cannot blow up, so it extends to all of $[0,T]$, and the family $\{u_n\}$ is bounded in $L^\infty_t L^2_x \cap L^2_t \dot H^1_x$ *uniformly in $n$*. That uniform bound is the heart of the matter.

**4. Compactness and passage to the limit.** From the uniform bound, extract a subsequence with $u_n \rightharpoonup u$ weakly-* in $L^\infty_t L^2_x$ and weakly in $L^2_t V$. The obstruction is the nonlinear term: weak convergence does not pass through a product. One needs **strong** convergence of $u_n$ in $L^2_t L^2_x$ (or $L^2$ on space-time), which Hopf obtains from a bound on the time derivative $\partial_t u_n$ in a weaker (negative-order) space, giving equicontinuity in time, combined with the spatial gradient bound. In modern language this is the **Aubin-Lions-Simon lemma**: bounded in $L^2_t V$, with $\partial_t u_n$ bounded in $L^2_t V'$, gives relative compactness in $L^2_t H$. The strong convergence lets the quadratic term pass to the limit, and the limit $u$ solves the weak equation.

**5. The energy inequality, not equality, survives the limit.** The norms in the energy identity are weakly lower-semicontinuous, so passing to the weak limit can only *decrease* the left-hand side relative to its $\limsup$. The clean per-$n$ energy *equality* therefore degrades to an *inequality* for the limit $u$. This is not a technical blemish: it is the precise functional-analytic signature of "we built $u$ by compactness, and compactness loses information." The lost information is exactly the small-scale information a regularity proof would need.

**Why the boundary complicates the energy method.** On the whole space (Leray) or the torus, one can differentiate freely and integrate by parts without boundary terms; vorticity is a convenient variable. On a bounded domain with no-slip walls:

- The pressure cannot be ignored at the level of higher estimates: $p$ is determined by a Neumann problem ($\Delta p = -\nabla\cdot[(u\cdot\nabla)u]$ with a boundary condition forced by $u|_{\partial\Omega}=0$), and boundary terms in integrations by parts no longer vanish for general test fields. Hopf's escape is to *test only against divergence-free fields vanishing on $\partial\Omega$*, which kills the pressure and the boundary terms simultaneously. The price is that the natural higher-regularity estimates (in particular the vorticity / enstrophy estimates that are so clean on $\mathbb{T}^d$) acquire boundary contributions and are genuinely harder. This is one reason the vorticity formulation, central to 2D regularity and to the Beale-Kato-Majda criterion, is more delicate on domains with boundary.
- The boundary breaks the scaling symmetry: the rescaling $u_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^2 t)$ does not map a fixed bounded $\Omega$ to itself. So the criticality bookkeeping below is a *local* statement (it concerns the behavior of a hypothetical singularity at an interior point, where one can rescale into the whole space), not a global symmetry of the boundary-value problem.

The boundary difficulty is, however, exactly why Hopf's contribution is load-bearing rather than cosmetic: he showed the energy method is robust enough to survive the loss of the whole-space symmetries, by leaning entirely on the one cancellation $((u\cdot\nabla)u,u)=0$ that does *not* need the boundary to be absent.

## Criticality placement

The norms Hopf controls are the energy norms, so the verdict is the project's central one. Using the criticality bookkeeper convention (`experiments/_shared/criticality.py`), under $u_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^2 t)$ the spatial Lebesgue norm scales as $\|u_\lambda\|_{L^q}=\lambda^{\,1-d/q}\|u\|_{L^q}$, exponent $a = 1 - d/q$.

| Quantity Hopf controls | norm | scaling exponent $a$ (d=3) | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| kinetic energy bound | $\|u\|_{L^2}$ | $a = 1 - 3/2 = -1/2$ | **supercritical** |
| dissipation bound | $\|\nabla u\|_{L^2} = \|u\|_{\dot H^1}$ | $a = 1 + 1 - 3/2 = +1/2$ | subcritical, but only in $L^2_t$, not all-time |

The energy norm $\|u\|_{L^2}$ has $a = -1/2 < 0$: it **decays** under the small-scale rescaling, so it gives *no control at the small scales where a singularity would form*. Running `audit_estimate` on this controlling norm returns the verdict `INSUFFICIENT_BY_ITSELF`: a supercritical controlling norm cannot, on its own, close a regularity statement, because regularity is a critical-or-subcritical statement (the critical Lebesgue exponent is $q=3$, where $a=0$, the Escauriaza-Seregin-Sverak endpoint).

The dissipation term $\nu\int_0^t\|\nabla u\|_{L^2}^2$ involves $\|u\|_{\dot H^1}$, whose spatial exponent is $+1/2$ (subcritical, good). But it is controlled *only in $L^2$ in time*, i.e. as a finite integral, not as an all-time pointwise bound. The natural critical quantity built from it would be $L^4_t \dot H^1_x$ or the Prodi-Serrin combination, and the energy gives neither: by interpolation the energy yields $u\in L^{p}_t L^q_x$ only on the supercritical line $2/p + 3/q = 3/2$ (the "energy line"), which sits strictly *above* the Prodi-Serrin scaling line $2/p+3/q=1$ by a fixed gap of $1/2$. That fixed gap of $1/2$, between $3/2$ and $1$, is the **supercriticality gap** made quantitative, and it is exactly what Hopf's construction does not close.

So Hopf places the existence theory precisely at the supercritical level (control B), and his note is the canonical statement of *the* bound we have. Everything in Architectures 2 and 3 is the attempt to add control that lives at $a\ge 0$, which Hopf's method structurally cannot supply.

## Against the three controls

**(A) 2D control.** Hopf's construction is dimension-agnostic: the energy cancellation $((u\cdot\nabla)u,u)=0$ holds in every dimension, so his theorem produces weak solutions in $d=2$ and $d=3$ identically. This is the correct behavior for an *existence* statement (weak solutions do exist in both). The 2D control bites not on existence but on *regularity*: in 2D the additional enstrophy bound ($\int|\omega|^2$ non-increasing, because there is no vortex-stretching term $\omega\cdot\nabla u$) upgrades the Hopf weak solution to a unique smooth one (Ladyzhenskaya 1959), whereas in 3D that upgrade is exactly what is missing. So Hopf passes control A in the right way: his method does *not* claim regularity, and therefore does not falsely predict either 2D or 3D smoothness. It is honest about delivering only existence. A method that used Hopf's energy bound to *assert* regularity would fail control A, because the same energy bound holds in 3D where regularity is open.

**(B) Supercriticality.** This is the primary tie-in and the verdict is unambiguous: the bound Hopf proves is supercritical (energy norm exponent $-1/2$ in $d=3$), as audited above. Hopf is the cleanest possible illustration of why control B is the ceiling: he extracts the maximum from the energy method, on the most general (bounded, no-slip) domain, and the maximum is still a supercritical bound. The construction *cannot* be pushed to a critical bound because the only coercive quantity it has access to is the energy, and the energy is supercritical. New genuinely critical control is required, and it is not available at the energy level. This is the supercriticality gap, and Hopf draws its lower edge.

**(C) Viscosity / Burgers control.** Hopf's method *uses* viscosity in an essential, visible place: the dissipation term $\nu\|\nabla u\|_{L^2}^2$ is what closes the energy estimate and provides the $L^2_t \dot H^1_x$ compactness. With $\nu=0$ (Euler) the gradient bound disappears, the Aubin-Lions compactness argument loses its spatial-regularity input, and the limit passage in the nonlinear term breaks. So Hopf is not blind to viscosity; on the contrary, the construction is built around it. (This is why convex-integration non-uniqueness, Architecture 5, is the more honest probe of how little the *weak* equation constrains the solution below the energy class: Hopf gives existence in the energy class using viscosity, and convex integration shows that just below that class, viscosity no longer rescues uniqueness.) Hopf passes control C.

Net: Hopf passes all three controls in the manner appropriate to an existence theorem. It does the right thing by *not* over-claiming, and it pins the energy bound at the supercritical level where control B says it must sit.

## What it gives / what it does not give

**Gives.**

- Global-in-time existence of a finite-energy weak solution for arbitrary divergence-free $L^2$ data, on a *bounded* domain with the physical no-slip boundary condition. This is the result that makes the theory applicable to real (walled) fluid domains, not just the idealized whole space or torus.
- A clean, reusable construction (Faedo-Galerkin) that became the standard machine for existence theorems in nonlinear PDE far beyond Navier-Stokes.
- The energy inequality in the bounded-domain setting, and with it the definition of the **Leray-Hopf class**: the function class $L^\infty_t L^2_x\cap L^2_t \dot H^1_x$ together with the energy inequality. This class is the arena for everything downstream (partial regularity, weak-strong uniqueness, the convex-integration non-uniqueness boundary).
- The isolation of the one cancellation $((u\cdot\nabla)u, u)=0$ as the load-bearing structural fact of the energy method.

**Does not give (the gap to regularity, stated precisely).**

- **No uniqueness.** Hopf's weak solution is not known to be unique in 3D. The Galerkin limit could in principle depend on the choice of basis and subsequence. (Sixty years later, Buckmaster-Vicol 2019 showed non-uniqueness does occur *below* the Leray-Hopf energy class, and Albritton-Brue-Colombo 2022 showed non-uniqueness *within* the Leray-Hopf class for forced Navier-Stokes. The unforced 3D case remains open.)
- **No regularity.** The weak solution is not known to be smooth, or even bounded, in 3D. It could have singular times. Hopf's bound controls only $L^\infty_t L^2_x\cap L^2_t\dot H^1_x$, and that is supercritical: it is compatible with worlds where $u$ concentrates at arbitrarily small scales.
- **No energy equality.** Only the inequality survives the limit, so anomalous dissipation is not excluded at this level.
- **No critical control.** The construction supplies nothing at scaling exponent $\ge 0$. The whole later program (Prodi-Serrin, BKM, ESS, Fujita-Kato, Koch-Tataru) is the search for critical control that Hopf's method cannot reach.

The honest one-line summary: Hopf proves *a* solution exists in the supercritical energy class and stops exactly where the supercriticality stops him. Everything after is the attempt to climb from the energy level ($a=-1/2$) to the critical level ($a=0$).

## Lineage and sharpest known form

**Builds on.**

- Leray 1934 (whole-space weak solutions by mollification and compactness): Hopf takes the existence idea and re-engineers the construction to handle bounded domains and no-slip walls via Galerkin projection rather than mollification.
- The Faedo-Galerkin philosophy (finite-dimensional projection plus a priori bound plus limit) and the Helmholtz-Weyl / Leray decomposition that supplies the divergence-free basis and eliminates the pressure.

**Built on it.**

- The Leray-Hopf class is the standard arena. **Ladyzhenskaya** (1959, 1969) developed the bounded-domain theory systematically, including the 2D uniqueness/regularity that uses the enstrophy bound absent in 3D. **Temam** ("Navier-Stokes Equations: Theory and Numerical Analysis") and **Constantin-Foias** ("Navier-Stokes Equations") are the canonical modern treatments of exactly Hopf's functional-analytic framework (the spaces $H$, $V$, the Stokes operator $A$, the Galerkin method).
- **Serrin** (1962) and **Prodi** (1959): weak-strong uniqueness inside the Leray-Hopf class. If one Leray-Hopf solution happens to satisfy a Prodi-Serrin condition ($u\in L^p_t L^q_x$, $2/p+3/q\le1$, $q>3$), it is unique and coincides with any strong solution. This is the conditional repair of the uniqueness gap Hopf left open.
- **Caffarelli-Kohn-Nirenberg** (1982): partial regularity of *suitable* weak solutions (a subclass of Leray-Hopf solutions satisfying a local energy inequality). The singular set has parabolic Hausdorff measure $\mathcal P^1=0$. This is the sharpest unconditional regularity statement, and it is a refinement living inside Hopf's class.
- **Escauriaza-Seregin-Sverak** (2003): the $L^\infty_t L^3_x$ endpoint continuation criterion, the critical-norm statement that says exactly what extra (critical) control would suffice. See [`escauriaza_seregin_sverak_2003.md`](escauriaza_seregin_sverak_2003.md).

**Sharpest known form (through 2025).**

- **Existence on domains** is settled and standard; the open questions Hopf left (3D uniqueness, 3D regularity) are *the* open questions and remain open.
- **Energy equality.** The Leray-Hopf inequality has been upgraded to *equality* under critical integrability hypotheses (Lions, Shinbrot; and refinements: $u\in L^4_t L^4_x$ suffices, and more generally $L^p_t L^q_x$ on appropriate lines), tying the equality to the same critical thresholds as regularity and to the Onsager-type analysis. The bare Hopf solution still has only the inequality.
- **Non-uniqueness boundaries.** Buckmaster-Vicol (2019) and Albritton-Brue-Colombo (2022) sharply locate where uniqueness fails relative to Hopf's class, which is the modern reading of "what Hopf's existence does not pin down." See the convex-integration boundary direction below.
- **Quantitative regularity.** Tao (2019) and Barker-Prange give quantitative versions of ESS, turning "a singularity forces the critical norm to blow up" into explicit (slowly growing) lower bounds. These are quantitative refinements of how far one must push *above* Hopf's supercritical bound to detect a singularity.
